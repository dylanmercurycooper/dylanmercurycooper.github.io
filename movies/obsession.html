Review · written 10 June 2026

Obsession — 9/10

Director: Curry Barker

Absolute Cinema (insert Scorsese meme)! I just got home from my second viewing of Obsession. Since my local cinema isn’t screening this film, I’ve had to go into central London to watch it twice now, and fun fact: on first viewing, Pedro Pascal was in our audience, on second, I was sat next to Louis Theroux. So, if you’ve ever wanted to see a celebrity in person, then I guess this is your sign to go to Picturehouse Central! Now, onto the film.

Obsession takes the classic moral tale of “be careful what you wish for”, weaves it into another familiar trope (“the crazily obsessed partner”), and yet still manages to deliver an intense, scary, and unique spin on both. Not once did I feel bored or unengaged—even in scenes which were predictable enough for me to know how they would play out. In essence, Obsession tells the tale of a spineless, soon-to-be friend-zoned guy named Bear, who is in love with his “friend” Nikki, both of whom are part of a group of four friends. I say “friend” in quotes because, from what the movie shows us, it doesn’t seem like Bear knows or understands Nikki well at all. On the way to the friend group’s weekly trivia night at the local bar, Bear buys a “One Wish Willow” for Nikki from a local hippie store that claims to grant one wish that will come true for whoever uses it. Bear intends to confess his feelings for Nikki, but he fails to do so at the bar. And even after driving her home and being prompted to tell her if he has feelings for her—in a sort of “let’s get this chapter over with” way—he is still unable to muster up the courage to do so. Defeated and frustrated at his own ineptitude, Bear uses the wish for himself, wishing for Nikki to love him more than anyone in the world. To his surprise, as well as impending detriment, the wish takes effect.

I think what resonated particularly strongly with me here is that the film revolves around a theme that permeates most of my ventures. It can be found in my research pursuits, my philosophical interests, and my political stances. That theme is autonomy. Autonomy seems to be something we all have an innate experience of. It feels central to how we perceive and engage with reality and links closely to the notion of free will: the idea that we have agency over how we navigate the world, and that if we will something, we can actively work towards achieving it. What would it mean, then, to have the power to effectively disengage someone else’s will or to actively modify someone’s mental or emotional states without their volition? Well, in Obsession, Bear does just this. While I have seen some discussion online about whether Nikki might have actually had feelings for Bear, I, for one, do not believe this was the case. From my perspective, before the wish is made, Nikki treats Bear in an obviously platonic way. Even when confronting him about his feelings towards her, she does so more to address the topic than to open the door to a potential relationship. Rather than facing even the possibility of rejection, then, Bear withholds his desire for Nikki’s affection and forces a wish upon her that goes against what I feel to be true: that she does not see him as a potential partner.

There also seems to be some debate around Bear’s moral status. I have heard from several people how they do not perceive Bear as a “bad guy” until quite late into the film, much later than when he actually made his wish. I find this a curious view. It effectively absolves Bear of his intent to force his will upon Nikki. I would be really interested to hear remarkably young and remarkably remarkable director Curry Barker’s viewpoint on this. What I particularly love about Curry’s writing here is that Bear never redeems himself. Usually, one would expect Hollywood to turn the flawed protagonist into someone who ends up “righting their wrongs”, but Obsession never does this. As remarked by Curry, part of this is also attributable to Bear’s actor, Michael Johnston, who overturned the script’s “redemption” of Bear at the end of the movie in favour of showing how, even after all of what he’d caused, he is still too cowardly to “do the right thing”—something which I think makes the story all the more human and realistic (or perhaps that’s just my cynical perception of humanity).

Another thing I want to touch on is the, in my mind, intentional exploitation of psychological tricks to engineer a sense of unease. The way Nikki moves, physically, once the wish has been forced upon her, seems inhuman. But not completely inhuman. Just on the cusp, invoking a sense of uncanny valley. Something which other films have done unintentionally (I’m looking at you, Will Smith’s Genie). Some of this is achieved by reversing footage to make the backwards motion look more fluid and quicker than it would be naturally. Other times, it is achieved through the script, where Nikki makes odd mistakes that no human would, such as stating that her cat died when it was in fact Bear’s. Furthermore, Nikki’s erratic nature creates an intense sense of: I do not know how this person is going to act. Having the feeling that a person does not share the same threshold for acceptable or unacceptable behaviour as the social norms humans are supposed to adhere to can create a visceral fear response in us. We don’t want to feel like we have no idea what someone is going to do. We like predictable, inferable behaviour. “Wish” Nikki demonstrates neither.

On a final note, I want to commend the film for scaring me in a way that films rarely do. It seems easy for films to use sudden loud noises to make me jump by appealing to my physiological responses. What isn’t easy, though, is to create psychological fear. The most horrific scene for me was one where nothing “seemingly scary”, at least visually, happens at all. Bear is in his car and calls the “One Wish Willow’s” customer service. While initially comedic, due to the absurdity of this wish actually working and having a hotline, it suddenly turns incredibly disturbing. Here, again, the film shows how selfish and callous Bear really is, as he asks for the chance to alter his wish. After being told this isn’t possible, Bear asks whether the wish is “real” for Nikki, the only time he really seems to address the huge moral issue he has created. Bear is then asked whether he wants to “talk” to Nikki. Confused, he asks what that means, and what follows are a couple of seconds of the real Nikki, seemingly conscious and aware of what is going on, but trapped in a body that is acting in ways she cannot control, screaming at the top of her lungs and incapable of changing what she is experiencing. That scene has haunted me since. And I absolutely love it.
