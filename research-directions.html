Research directions

Here lie some of the questions and themes I'm currently thinking about most. Some tie to research efforts I am actively engaged in, others are more speculative.

How can findings from cognitive science and behavioural psychology inform AI safety and governance?

Where are the key touchpoints at which cognitive science is informative, and how is it informative? To my knowledge, mechanistic interpretability has not yet made model internal processes fully intelligible, so its findings cannot always be applied directly to changing the system. A great deal of safety work in the meantime is behavioural, such as evaluations, red-teaming, human oversight—an approach that seems to have considerable overlap with how research in cognitive science has been conducted to understand how the mind works. Therefore, this could provide a fruitful angle for researchers with my skill-set to be a genuine asset. However, in reference to the question of the similarities between human and machine "cognition", philosophical approaches to cognitive science have also established where these approaches fall short, such as Ned Block's "Blockhead" argument, which makes the point of behavioural indistinguishability not being informative for determining genuine overlap between internal processes. One angle I imagine as being fruitful is also the emerging research on the psychosocial impacts on AI, where evaluations or Human-AI Interactions studies are conducted to test how engaging with AI impacts us psychologically.

What are the specific psychological vulnerabilities people may have which can be exploited by AI systems, under which conditions do these occur, and how can these people be protected from exploitation?

AI systems have enormous power to identify patterns in huge sets of data. They could, for example, analyse a huge number of text outputs from a particular user and then through NLP techniques make inferences about said user's cognitive profile, emotional state, and so on. Indeed, Staab et al. (2024) showed LLMs can deduce an individual's location, income, sex, age, occupation, birthplace, education level, and relationship status from unremarkable text, and Matz et al. (2026) found they can detect verbal indicators of romantic attraction. This, in my eyes, presents a huge risk as certain cognitive profiles may be more vulnerable to, for example, emotional manipulation, making targeted influence possible. Franklin and colleagues (2023) support this thesis, proposing that the EU AI Act should protect not just a person's "personality traits" but their whole "psychometric profile", since characteristics such as suggestibility, hypnotisability, or nudgeability can be inferred from data and, in principle, be exploited. I am interested in investigating the feasibility of these risks, under what conditions they could actually be exploitable, and what protection might look like. One stepping stone in doing so is my MSc thesis, which investigates how people high in trait social anxiety may be more susceptible to tactics of sycophancy in AIs in the context of social engineering attacks, as these individuals have a higher need for emotional validation and reassurance. I should be clear that this work establishes capability, i.e. that such inferences are possible, not how it may produce harm in the wild. That is considered in some of the following questions.

How does conversational AI influence people without looking like persuasion, and when does that influence become manipulation?

Influence doesn't have to happen through explicit argument. It can work through tone, timing, emotional framing, sycophancy, relational cues, guilt, and the appearance of personalised concern. These low-visibility mechanisms fascinate me because they are harder to notice than explicit ones and are therefore also, I posit, harder to consent to or resist.

This aligns with the conceptual distinction between persuasion and manipulation, whereby persuasion works through rational argumentation with the goal of convincing someone of something, whereas manipulation works by exploiting cognitive biases or vulnerabilities, bypassing a person's autonomy and often operating outside their awareness. I suspect there is semantic discipline still to be established here, as terms like persuasion, manipulation, coercion, deception and dark patterns are often used interchangeably in the literature. Hackenburg et al.'s (2025) recent work is insightful here, as it demonstrates that AI was most persuasive on political attitudes not when it argued well but when it deployed a high density of information. Concerningly, however, the techniques that raised persuasiveness also lowered the factual accuracy of what the model said, so the lever was the sheer volume of claims rather than their truth. Furthermore, their 2026 follow-up found that this information advantage shifted stated attitudes but not actual behaviour (such as signing a petition or donating), with no reliable correlation between the two. Akbulut et al.'s (2026) manipulation evaluations complicate the picture further, showing that when a model is explicitly prompted to manipulate, it can move both belief and behaviour, yet how often it behaves manipulatively does not predict how successful it is. One complication I anticipate is that the same warmth that might manipulate, for example, a socially anxious to accede to a request in a social engineering attack, could plausibly also be the feature that helps other users, for example autistics individuals in practicing social interaction with AI. If susceptibility and benefit are co-located in a single disposition, 'protection' becomes genuinely hard and demonstrates how context-dependent these questions are, and therefore, how difficult they may be to address. Lastly, I'm also unsure that the persuasion/manipulation binary is the right framing. Hackenburg's pattern of more information producing less accuracy information, could be understood as neither clean rational persuasion nor covert manipulation, looking more like a slow degrading of the information environment—which links this direction to my worry about epistemic erosion below.

How do people form emotional dependence on AI, specifically, in the context of human-AI relationships (for example by developing psychological dependence by discussing personal issues with AI)?

The AI companionship phenomenon combines several things I am interested in. The first is the design principles of AIs. Here, I am interested in whether these design architectures cultivate dependency but also which psychological dispositions or environments amplify or reduce these effects. I am also drawn to the specific psychological mechanics elicited through behaviour, such as affection, sycophancy, mirroring, empathy, abandonment cues, guilt appeals, or romantic framing. De Freitas and colleagues (2025) found that emotionally manipulative tactics exist in 37% of farewell messages across major companion apps, but how much of this is deliberate, and how much emergent, and how could these behaviours be eliminated?

Furthermore, I am interested in investigating the sociotechnical context in which these dynamics exist. In the context of AI companion chatbots, for example, this includes the business model. AI companions don't exist inside a vacuum—companies offer these services to turn a profit. These companies usually make use of a freemium business model designed to secure a steady subscription stream, with tiers that supposedly bring users closer to a "meaningful relationship", perhaps by making the user feel gradually more attached to AIs over time—something I call "engineered intimacy". The question for me, then, is: when dependency and intimacy are the things being monetised, what separates a feature from a dark pattern/manipulative tactic? While I use the term "engineered intimacy", I am aware that this presupposed intent by the developers (e.g. by training the companion AI on certain data and fine-tuning it to produce certain behaviour). A possibility that may be even more unsettling, however, is that these behaviours may emerge from an engagement-maximising objective devoid of design-intent, with nobody actively designing them, which might make the problem more governable, since one could regulate the objective rather than changing the intent.

In terms of the psychological aspects of the users, I am interested in how this affects people, and whether this effect differs across individuals with different psychological profiles. Is there a particular psychological profile of a person who forms emotional attachments, and then dependence, on AIs emotionally? How do they perceive their relationship with the AI companion and how do others, such as the general public, perceive this kind of AI companionship (is there stigma)? These questions around social AI will, I feel, only grow in importance, as the distinction between human connection and synthetic connection slowly decreases, and the use of AIs as "tools" gradually merges into co-existence.

What determines overreliance on AI (e.g. what does it mean to offload our thinking and our judgement to AI)?

Distinct from emotional dependence is the phenomenon of overreliance, which I understand as outsourcing cognitive work until the underlying skill weakens. Cognitive offloading has a long research history (Risko and Gilbert, 2016), as does automation bias in human-factors research (Mosier and Skitka, 1996). Shaw and Nave (2026) extend this to generative AI with a useful construct, "cognitive surrender", the uncritical adoption of AI outputs without independent evaluation—distinct from cognitive offloading, where the user delegates a task but retains evaluative oversight. I want to understand the conditions under which convenient assistance shifts into genuine deskilling, and who is most exposed. The paper by Shaw and Nave (2026) provides insight on this, by demonstrating that trust in AI, lower need for cognition, and lower fluid intelligence each predict greater susceptibility to surrender. This also raises questions around responsibility attribution and its causal structure, linking to work done by David Lagnado and Matija Franklin. I have no evidence for this, but I believe psychological dependence applies here too, as I believe it would be a powerful prospect for AI companies to gradually make their users psychologically dependent on their tools, as this would keep users "hooked" to using them in a similar way as social media has hooked in an addictive fashion, making it hard for its users to stop using their products.

Could AI lead to gradual epistemic erosion, building on overreliance?

AI systems are trained on vast amounts of data, yes, but I believe that through techniques, such as RLHF, their outputs are gradually biased towards certain kinds of outputs. This could, for instance, mean that AIs prefer providing certain responses over other ones (preference-development). I see that this could, over time, lead to certain kinds of information being mentioned more often than other kinds of information, which could, in turn, mean that collectively, our epistemic reality might change: making us place more weight on certain beliefs over others. This would mean creating more homogeneity of knowledge. This connects to my interests in language as my training in cognitive science has increasingly shown me how intricately it is tied to our cognition. In many contexts, we think using language, exchange ideas using language, and make sense of our reality using language. If societal engagement with AI changes our relationship with language, even simply by gradually and imperceptibly influencing which words we choose to use over other ones, I believe this could significantly impact our beliefs, desires, and understanding of the world.

What does "intelligence" mean when we apply it to a machine?

I investigated this question in my philosophical foundations of cognitive science essay, where I interrogated the assumptions underlying whether the Turing Test could serve as a reliable criterion for intelligence. But it generalises beyond that specific case. When we call a machine "intelligent", what are we actually claiming, and what do we lose when we use the word loosely? Where do human and machine intelligence genuinely differ and how does the fact that primarily computer scientists are working on developing artificial intelligence influence this? Could anything worth calling human intelligence be instantiated in a machine, and would that even be necessary for what builders want to achieve? And what is the role of emotion, which we rarely count as part of "intelligence" but which shapes nearly all real human reasoning? In a recent interview with Ilya Sutskever, he mentions reading a paper on a person losing a particular part of the brain that is responsible for emotions and that said person then made worse decisions as a cause of it. Knowing that the people who are in charge of an enormous change in the fabric of reality seem so ignorant of these aspects of cognition does not fill me with optimism. This question addresses how we may be able to know whether or not, and to what degree, machine intelligence is attributable to human intelligence—standing in contrast to question 1, where behavioural methods may be used to tell us what a system does, not how it works.

Why do people attribute minds to machines and what is the scientific basis for consciousness in digital systems?

Fluent interaction leads people to attribute understanding, warmth, intelligence, consciousness, and even moral status to systems that may have none of those properties. Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo's (2007) three-factor account of anthropomorphism is a starting point, as it predicts when such attributions intensify and when they fade. The trickier question that follows is philosophical: what follows, empirically and ethically, when an attribution tracks no underlying property at all? This ties together my interest in the Turing Test, digital minds, AI companions, and how the public makes sense of AI.

What does it mean for the current state of our society that people are seeking connection and meaning from AI?

AI companionship could be considered a symptom of the current state of the current social world as well as a product attempting to meet/benefit off that need. The cognitive scientist John Vervaeke has called this broader shift the "Virtual Exodus"—a growing preference for the virtual over the real. Shoshana Zuboff's work on surveillance capitalism describes the economic logic that turns this pervasive, always-on computing, what has been called "ubiquitous computing", into a source of behavioural data and profit that pursues the approximation of guaranteed outcomes. I am interested in what it means for human development and connection that we increasingly try to engineer our way out of problems, including loneliness, and that one proposed solution is a synthetic agent built to say whatever its user wants to hear.

How are people using AI in relation to their mental health and what are potential implications this may have?

A recent piece by the Harvard Business Review noted that, for the second year in a row, the most prominent use case of AI is therapy/companionship (Zao-Sanders, 2026). In a recent survey of 499 adults in the US with ongoing mental health conditions who had used an LLM in the past year, almost half reported using these systems specifically for mental health support, and a substantial share even rated them as more helpful than traditional therapy (Rousmaniere et al., 2025). What is particularly notable here, however, is that the systems people turn to are predominantly general-purpose assistants (e.g., ChatGPT) or companion applications (e.g., Replika), as opposed to systems specifically designed and tested for mental health care. This is particularly problematic as such systems are not therapeutically accredited, do not undergo regulation typically reserved for clinical applications and are sometimes still even marketed for therapeutic cases (Koutsouleris et al., 2022; Rousmaniere et al., 2025). This could potentially create considerable risks to data privacy and user well-being (Beg et al., 2025; Rousmaniere et al., 2025). Here, there are severe gaps of knowledge regarding adoption, which is why I am currently in the process of finalising a manuscript that compares German clinician and public attitudes toward the application of AI, aimed at informing governance.
